Why 'Other Ingredients' Order Matters on a Label

Most buyers read the active ingredients on a supplement label and skip the "other ingredients" list entirely. That is a mistake, because the other-ingredients list is where the binders, fillers, coatings, colours, and preservatives live — and the order they appear in is supposed to tell you how much of each is in the capsule. The word "supposed" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

In principle, the other-ingredients list follows the same rule as the active list: ingredients in descending order of weight. The first item is the one there is most of, and the last is the one there is least of. That rule lets a careful reader work out whether a capsule is mostly active ingredient or mostly filler, and whether the colour or the preservative is a trace amount or a meaningful one. In practice, the rule is enforced far more loosely for other ingredients than for active ones, and the list is short enough that small differences in order carry little information.

The first reason the order matters less than it should is that many other ingredients are used in such tiny amounts that their order is essentially arbitrary. A flow agent at 0.5 percent and a coating at 0.4 percent can swap places between batches without anything changing, and the label may or may not be updated to match. Reading the order as a precise ranking of tiny quantities is reading more into the list than the list can support.

The second reason is that brands can arrange the list to flatter the product. A filler a buyer might dislike can be pushed down the list, while a benign-sounding ingredient can be pushed up, and the list will still be technically in descending order if the amounts are close enough. The third reason is that the list changes between markets. A product sold in the United States and in Europe may carry different other-ingredients lists for the same formula, because the two markets name and order ingredients differently. The formula has not changed; the paperwork has. A botanical eye-health formula, for example, can look like two different products on paper, and a review of a botanical eye-health formula helps you see past the naming differences to the formula underneath.

How should you read the list, then? Look for the presence of ingredients you care about, not their position. If an ingredient you want to avoid is on the list at all, its position does not make it harmless. Look at the length of the list as a whole: a short list usually means a simpler product, and a long list usually means a more processed one. And remember that the order is a rough guide, not a precise measurement — useful for spotting the biggest components, useless for ranking the smallest ones.
